Ace bias, retreat thresholds, and escort doctrine

Short answer:
- Yes, a T1 Ace is intentionally favored in a neutral-air 1v1 or small-flight encounter against 1939 rifle-caliber fighters.
- That bias is not a bug if the larger system still makes Aces scarce, exhaustible, detectable, and strategically containable.
- The setting should work like this: the Ace wins the duel, but the network decides the campaign.

Why the Ace is favored:
- The maneuver gap is real. A fighter must preserve airspeed, turn radius, and energy state. The Ace can brake to a hover, reverse, and pivot without stall behavior.
- Snap breaks firing geometry. A plane lines up solutions through prediction; Snap invalidates prediction inside 100 yd.
- Flick is naturally lethal to aircraft. Planes are large, fragile, high-speed machines with exposed pilot, radiator, engine, fuel, and control surfaces.
- Rifle-caliber guns being below barrier threshold means early fighters cannot count on a pass translating into a kill.

What keeps that from becoming silly:
- The Ace is only favored once alert, ramped, and not already deep in a defended web.
- The Ace has limited Snap budget and should treat each Snap as precious operational capital, not casual movement.
- The Ace is slower in straight line and cannot force pursuit on an unwilling fighter.
- The Ace still has to see, solve, and time the shot. He is deadly, not omniscient.

Useful analogy:
- Do not think '1 Tiger = 4 Shermans.' That analogy is too static and too duel-focused.
- Better: 1 Ace is like a compressed combined-arms raid package. It can humiliate isolated conventional units, but it can still be fixed, denied, tired out, and killed by a prepared system.
- So the right myth in-world is something like: one Ace can ruin a squadron's day, but not rewrite a theater by itself.

Retreat threshold in neutral air or over the ocean:
- A lone Ace should start thinking about disengagement once he loses surprise, expends 1-2 Snaps, or realizes the enemy is not committing stupidly.
- Over open water, the Ace has little concealment and no terrain tricks. That makes long contact dangerous even if the fighters cannot kill him directly.
- The real danger is not the five planes already seen. It is the unseen vectoring chain: more fighters, shadowers, destroyers, acoustic pickets, coastal AA, or a prepared jammer corridor ahead.
- So the Ace absolutely can retreat, but should retreat early rather than heroically. His survival is worth more than one extra plane kill.

Can the Ace retreat successfully?
- Over land: yes, often. Trees, ridgelines, towns, cloud base, and line-of-sight breaks all help. Snap plus terrain is a real escape tool.
- Over ocean: yes, but less comfortably. He cannot hide; he can only break geometry, drop altitude, reverse unexpectedly, and try to exit before more counters arrive.
- In practical doctrine, the Ace retreats not because five 1939 fighters are certain death, but because five fighters mean contact has been established and the larger kill web is waking up.

How worried should an Ace be in neutral space?
- Against 1-2 early fighters: low fear, high caution.
- Against 4-6 disciplined fighters keeping speed and spacing: moderate concern, because they can force time loss and reveal position.
- Against repeated relays of fighters or any sign of coordinated ground control: serious concern, mission probably aborted.
- Against cannon fighters, heavy MG concentration, or known jammer belt: immediate operational danger.

German doctrine should be:
- Aces do not stay to prove superiority.
- Aces kill the one aircraft that overcommits, then reposition or disengage.
- Recon Aces in particular should prioritize information and survival over tally.
- Aces should assume every minute of visible contact is compounding risk geometrically.

Should Aces be escorted by planes?
- Usually yes, for some missions.
- Not because the escort wins the Ace's duel for him, but because escorts solve problems the Ace does not.
- Friendly fighters extend scouting range, spot interceptors first, report the air picture, discourage enemy loitering, and keep conventional aircraft from simply shadowing the Ace to his route home.
- Escort fighters also matter during the Ace's vulnerable phases: transit, landing, takeoff, feeding, transfer, and post-sortie fatigue.

Should Aces be escorted by T2s?
- Usually no in the air, unless the mission specifically needs sacrificial noise or mass.
- T2s are too loud, too fragile, too maintenance-heavy, and too clumsy to accompany a T1 cleanly on recon.
- They advertise the package, slow planning, and create extra rescue/logistics burdens.
- They are better used in a separate role: local breakthrough support, decoy activity, saturation attacks, or fixed-position defense.

Best German planning model:
- Lone T1 for stealth recon or surgical strike.
- T1 plus conventional fighter escort for deeper penetration or when enemy fighters are expected.
- T1 plus ground-controlled route planning, prepared recovery site, and rapid debrief.
- T2s used elsewhere in the operation, not glued to the T1 unless Germany is desperate or doctrinally overconfident.

Clean doctrinal sentence:
- A German Ace should fear not the first five fighters, but the fact that five fighters mean the enemy has found him and the real battle is about to begin.
