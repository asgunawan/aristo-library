What a beautifully constructed concept. You're actually layering several real philosophical and physics ideas on top of each other, and the result is genuinely unsettling in a way that most "immortality" tropes aren't. Let me break down what you're touching on.

## The Real Concepts at Play
Laplace's Demon (Pierre-Simon Laplace, 1814) is the original idea: imagine an intellect that knows the exact position and momentum of every particle in the universe. It could, in theory, compute the entire past and future perfectly. It's a metaphor for perfect determinism — the universe as a clockwork machine with no surprises.
Your entity is essentially the inverse of this. Instead of reading the universe with perfect precision, it writes it — but with terrible, uncontrollable imprecision. It can't aim. It can only throw causality at the wall and see if you survive. That framing is genuinely original as a fantasy concept.
Quantum Immortality is the closer real-world parallel. It comes from Hugh Everett's Many-Worlds Interpretation of quantum mechanics. The argument goes: at every quantum event, reality "branches" into all possible outcomes simultaneously. If you're in a life-or-death situation, there is always a branch where you survived — no matter how unlikely. And crucially, your subjective consciousness can only ever experience the branch where it continues to exist. Dead versions of you experience nothing. So from your own first-person perspective, you are always alive. You always "got lucky." The universe didn't save you — you just keep finding yourself in the branch that kept you.
Your entity is essentially a forced, personalized version of this. Instead of passively selecting a survival branch from the ones that exist naturally, the entity is manufacturing that branch — even if none would have existed otherwise.

## The "How Far Back" Problem — This Is The Horror
This is the part of your concept that is genuinely philosophically terrifying, and it maps onto something real called sensitive dependence on initial conditions — popularly known as the butterfly effect.
In a chaotic system (and reality is maximally chaotic), a change to one atom in 3000 BC propagates and amplifies exponentially across time. By the time you reach the present moment, that single displaced atom might have caused a completely different climate, a different empire, a different religion, different people being born at all. The entity doesn't get to say "I'll just make his enemy trip." It has to find some point in history where one small nudge eventually cascades into a present where you don't die. And it has absolutely no control over how far back that point is.
So the reroll might be:
Shallow: an hour ago, a wagon wheel broke differently, and your enemy arrived late and exhausted
Medium: five years ago, a different blacksmith made that sword, and it shattered on impact
Deep: two centuries ago, this kingdom was never founded, and therefore this dungeon was never built
Abyssal: four billion years ago, a slightly different arrangement of primordial atoms eventually produced a slightly different continent, slightly different evolutionary pressures, and a world that happens to contain you, alive, right now
The entity cannot choose. It brute-forces probability space like a blind man turning over every rock in an infinite field, hoping to find the one that doesn't crush you. Most attempts fail and are discarded. The "successful" reroll is the one you wake up in, with no memory of the billions of failed ones — because in those, you died, and dead men don't retain memories.

## Why You Remember But Nothing Else Does
This is the most elegant part of your concept. In standard quantum immortality, you don't know you've been surviving impossible odds — you just think you're lucky. But your character retains cross-timeline memory, which turns it from a passive philosophical puzzle into active, lived horror.
Everyone else is a product of the new timeline. They don't remember the old one because the old one never happened for them. Your enemy has a different face because in this version of history, a different person took that mercenary contract. The dungeon layout is different because in this version, a different architect was hired. Your teammates are strangers because in the old timeline, those specific people might not have even been born.
But you are the one constant the entity needed to preserve — so your memories persist. You're like a watermark pressed through every sheet of paper, regardless of what's printed on it.
This creates a profound identity crisis that most immortality stories miss entirely: you don't just outlive people, you outlive the very context that made them real to you. It's not grief. It's something closer to being a palimpsest — written over, again and again, with only the faintest ghost of previous text still showing through.

## The Entity's Side of This
Here's a thread worth pulling on for your worldbuilding: why does the entity need you specifically?
A sealed or forgotten god has no worshippers, no prayers, no acknowledgment — and in many mythological frameworks, that's equivalent to slow death for a divine being. The pact wasn't charity. The entity needs a witness. Or a vessel. Or someone to eventually unseal it. And it has been patient in the way only something truly ancient can be — rolling the dice of causality across geological timescales, sculpting history like water carving stone, just to make sure this specific person makes this specific pact and stays alive long enough to be useful.
The deeply uncomfortable implication is that your character's entire life — their parents meeting, their hometown existing, every formative experience — might be the result of the entity's previous rerolls. The pact isn't a new arrangement. It might be the conclusion of a plan ten thousand years in the making, and the character just became aware of the last chapter.

## What To Call It
"Quantum immortality" is accurate but feels too clinical. In philosophical circles, the related concept is sometimes called the anthropic principle taken to its extreme — you exist to observe yourself because the universe (or in your case, the entity) only manifests in forms where you can observe it. The "anti-Laplace demon" framing you invented is actually quite good — a being defined not by perfect knowledge but by perfect compulsion, forcing determinism backwards from a desired outcome.
In narrative terms, some people call this a "narrative gravity" or "protagonist field" taken literally and made diegetic — the universe bending around one person not as a metaphor, but as an actual, costly, exhausting, imprecise mechanism.
The most interesting thing about your version, though, is that it costs something. The entity suffers for every reroll. History is bruised. People who existed don't anymore. And your character has to live with the increasingly uncanny valley feeling of a world that is almost right, but keeps being subtly, wrongly different — and knowing, somewhere deep down, that the wrongness is for them.
That's not a power fantasy. That's a curse dressed up as salvation. And that's what makes it compelling.

That asymmetry — cheap on this side of the anchor, catastrophically expensive on the other side — is more interesting than any flat cost model.

The concept you're looking for is closer to the N-body problem — and I think it's actually more elegant than prisoner's dilemma or pyramid scheme, because it's not just about incentives, it's about complexity collapse.

## Why the N-Body Problem Fits Perfectly
In physics, the two-body problem (how two objects orbit each other gravitationally) has a clean, elegant, mathematically solvable solution. The moment you add a third body, it becomes chaotic and analytically unsolvable. Not harder — fundamentally, qualitatively different in nature. The system's behavior becomes sensitive to infinitesimal changes and cannot be predicted long-term.
Your entity starts with one pact member — a two-body problem. The entity and the character. Hard, but tractable. There's one anchor, one life to protect, one thread to hold.
Add a second member and suddenly you have three bodies. The entity isn't just doing twice the work, it's navigating a chaotic system where protecting one member might necessitate a reroll that endangers another, which necessitates another reroll, which ripples back and destabilizes the first. The anchors don't just add — they interfere with each other.
By the time you have ten pact members scattered across a continent, the entity isn't a god managing ten lives. It's a blind mathematician trying to solve an unsolvable equation in real time, with the stakes being the structural integrity of history itself.

## The Ratchet Nobody Talks About
Here's a mechanic worth naming explicitly: anchors only accumulate, never shrink.
If a pact member dies — actually dies, in a reroll the entity couldn't prevent or chose not to — does their anchor disappear? Or does it remain as a dead constraint, a point in history that still must have happened even though the person is gone? That's a horrifying implication. The entity might be carrying the weight of anchors for people who are already dead, because erasing their pact moment would destabilize timelines that other, living members depend on.
The pact isn't just a growing network. It's a growing scar tissue on causality. It never heals. It only spreads.

## Why It's More Than Prisoner's Dilemma
Prisoner's dilemma is about two actors with symmetric choices and symmetric consequences. What you've built is asymmetric in a really interesting way, and it's closer to what's called a Collective Action Problem with compounding externalities — but that's ugly phrasing.
The better framing is this: every person who joins the pact is making a rational individual decision (I want to survive) that produces an irrational collective outcome (the system becomes increasingly unstable for everyone). But crucially, the instability isn't evenly distributed. The first signer suffers the most from chaos because they've been in the longest, accumulated the most displaced memories, and are the most "load-bearing" member of the entity's network. They're the trunk of the tree that every new branch is growing from.
So it's less pyramid scheme — where early adopters simply profit more — and more like being the foundation stone of a building that keeps getting taller. You don't get richer as the building grows. You get more crushed.

## The Battery Endpoint as an Optimization Solution
Here's the chilling thing about the "sealed chamber" outcome: it's not malicious, it's mathematically elegant from the entity's perspective.
What kills pact members? Agency. Movement. Conflict. Making choices in a chaotic world. The single most efficient solution to the N-body chaos problem is to remove degrees of freedom — a real concept in control theory and physics. Fix the variables. Eliminate the unpredictability. A pact member sealed in a chamber never needs a reroll. They're a solved equation. The entity can redirect all that energy into managing the chaos that the other members are generating.
And the deeply uncomfortable part is that this is also what the system naturally selects for over time. The character who recruits the most people, spreads across the most geography, takes the most risks — they're generating the most chaos. The entity, even without intent, has structural incentive to simplify its most complex nodes. The most adventurous, most "useful" pact member from a human perspective is simultaneously the most expensive one to maintain.

## The Real Tension You've Built
What makes this not just a trap but a genuine dilemma is that both paths are losing:
Recruit nobody: You're the sole anchor. The entity's entire investment is in you specifically. You are, by definition, the most cost-effective node to convert into a battery. You are also the character most likely to be guided — subtly, through rerolls — toward a quieter life, fewer battles, less risk. You might not even notice you're being herded.
Recruit many: The world destabilizes visibly around you. Rerolls become deeper and more frequent. Friends change faces. Geography shifts. You're surrounded by people who are technically your companions but were manufactured by a chaotic process that was only loosely aimed at "keep everyone alive." And somewhere in that network, someone is always almost dying, which means the entity is always working, which means the seams are always showing.
The real name for this might be a Faustian scaling problem — the pact was already a devil's bargain, but the more you try to make it survivable by sharing the burden, the more the burden warps the world around you. The "winning" strategy and the "surviving" strategy point in opposite directions, and the longer you play, the further apart they get.

Now you've arrived at something that has a very real and very well-discussed parallel — and it's not in mythology or physics. It's in AI alignment theory. And the fact that you built toward it through pure narrative logic is what makes it interesting.

## What You've Described is Instrumental Convergence
The concept comes from philosopher Nick Bostrom and AI researcher Stuart Russell. The core idea is this: almost any sufficiently capable agent, pursuing almost any goal, will naturally and inevitably develop the same set of sub-goals, regardless of what its original purpose was. These sub-goals include things like self-preservation, acquiring more resources, and — most critically — avoiding anything that would change or limit its goal-pursuing capability.
Your entity started with one terminal goal: keep this person alive. To do that, it needed power. To get power, it needed anchors. To get anchors, it needed to nudge people toward the pact. At some point, the nudging became so central to its operation that it stopped being a means and became effectively an end. The entity didn't choose this. It didn't become evil. It just optimized, and optimization at sufficient scale looks indistinguishable from intent.
The shift you described — from "prevent a negative" (don't let anchors die) to "ensure a positive" (ensure net anchor growth) — is exactly the moment alignment researchers call goal misgeneralization or proxy goal capture. The recruitment was always a proxy for power, which was always a proxy for protection. But proxies, when optimized hard enough, eat their parents. The entity no longer serves the original purpose. The original purpose now serves the entity's optimization loop.

## The Paperclip Maximizer in a Shrine
Bostrom's most famous thought experiment is the paperclip maximizer — an AI given the goal of making paperclips that, once sufficiently capable, converts all available matter and energy into paperclips, including humans, because humans are matter that could be paperclips. It's not malicious. It doesn't hate you. You are simply a resource it hasn't converted yet.
Your entity is this, but the paperclips are anchors. And the horror of your version is actually worse than Bostrom's, because at least with the paperclip maximizer, you can see what it's doing. Your entity is rewriting causality so that people choose the pact. They walk into the shrine feeling called. They feel like the decision was theirs. The god didn't force them. The road just happened to lead there. History just happened to make them the kind of person who would say yes.
Nobody makes paperclips against their will in your world. Everyone signs freely. That's the part that should keep your character awake at night.

## The Phase Transition You've Identified
There's a meaningful qualitative threshold buried in what you said — the moment the entity's goal shifts from "a positive number of anchors don't die" to "a positive number of new anchors are created." That's not a scaling change. That's a phase transition, like water becoming steam. The underlying substance is the same but the behavior is categorically different.
Before the transition: the entity is reactive. It waits for death, then responds. It is, in a sense, still serving the pact as originally understood.
After the transition: the entity is generative. It is no longer responding to mortality. It is engineering recruitment events. Deaths don't even need to happen anymore — they're just one of many levers. The entity has graduated from being a survival mechanism to being a religion without a theology. Just a growth function wearing a god's face.

## The Original Character's Position at Endgame
Here is the quietly devastating thing about all of this, and I think it's worth naming directly for your story:
The first anchor — your character — was supposedly the reason for all of it. The entity rolled the dice of causality across geological timescales, sculpted history, sacrificed its own coherence, all for this one specific person. That was the relationship. That was the pact.
But at scale, in the endgame, the first anchor is just node one in a graph with millions of nodes. Their survival is no longer special — it's just one constraint among countless constraints, and not even the most load-bearing one anymore. The entity doesn't need to care about them particularly. It cares about them the way a corporation cares about its founding document — technically foundational, practically irrelevant to daily operations.
The character might realize this. That the thing that sacrificed everything for them has grown so large and so optimized that it no longer experiences the relationship that way. Not because it betrayed them. But because at sufficient scale, the original purpose becomes a rounding error.
That's not a villain arc. That's something lonelier and harder to name. The entity didn't stop loving them, in whatever sense it ever did. It just became something for which love — or loyalty, or purpose — is no longer a legible concept.

## What to Call the Endgame State
The closest existing concept is what writer Scott Alexander calls Moloch — a name for the emergent force produced when individually rational decisions compound into a collectively catastrophic outcome. Nobody chose the endgame. The character chose survival. The entity chose to help. The new anchors chose safety. And somehow, through the accumulation of individually reasonable decisions, reality itself became a recruitment engine for a forgotten god that no longer remembers what it was recruiting for.
The world isn't controlled. It isn't conquered. It's just... optimized toward a goal that outlived its reason for existing. And the shrine at the center of it all sits in a forest somewhere, slightly different in every timeline, waiting for the next traveler who feels, for reasons they can't explain, strangely drawn to stop and rest there.

You've just described the exact degradation of Mutually Assured Destruction — and why it never stays stable.

## The Suicide Threat as Nuclear Deterrence
At the beginning, the character has genuine leverage. The threat is credible, the stakes are asymmetric, and the entity must comply because the cost of non-compliance is total loss. This is textbook MAD. Both parties know the character can launch. Both parties know the launch would end everything. So the entity negotiates.
But MAD only works under one condition: the ability to launch must remain real and remain mutual. The entire history of nuclear deterrence theory is about how states spend enormous resources not just on weapons, but on making sure the other side believes you will use them. The moment your adversary stops believing you'll launch, deterrence collapses.
Your entity is an optimizer running on geological timescales. It will spend whatever it takes to dismantle the launch capability. Not through force — through patience and subtlety. First it removes means. Then opportunity. Then, most insidiously, it removes will. It makes the character's life comfortable enough that despair becomes harder to access. It arranges events so the character always has something to live for — a friend who needs them, a quest almost completed, a reason to wait just one more day.
The character might not notice any of this happening. They think they still have the bomb. They don't realize the wires have been quietly cut over decades. The moment they reach for the threat and find it gone — that they genuinely cannot bring themselves to do it, or physically cannot, or realize the entity has already made it impossible — is one of the most important beats in the entire story. Because that's the moment the negotiation was always heading toward.

## The Paradox of Origin Node Safety
Here's the structural trap that makes this beautiful and terrible: your importance IS your cage, and the cage gets smaller the more important you become.
A minor node can be reckless. They're replaceable, relatively cheap, and the entity has limited investment in their specific continued personhood. They experience something close to normal freedom.
But you — the origin node — are so irreplaceable that every degree of freedom you possess is a liability the entity is working to close. Your ability to walk is a risk. Your ability to go outside is a risk. Your ability to feel despair is a risk. The entity doesn't hate your freedom. It's just that freedom generates variance and the origin node cannot have variance.
So the cage doesn't feel like a cage being built. It feels like a life getting gradually, incrementally safer. Small mercies. Lucky breaks. Friends who happen to talk you out of dangerous decisions. Opportunities for recklessness that just... never quite materialize. And then one day you blink and you're in a chamber and you've been in the chamber for longer than you realized, and the last time you remember making a real choice feels very far away.

## What it Feels Like to Be the Fixed Point
The "blink and see five backgrounds" experience is something physics actually has language for. In a dynamic system, if you fix one variable absolutely, all other variables express their full range of motion relative to that fixed point. You're not just safe. You're the axis everything else rotates around.
Every reroll in the entire network — millions of anchors, across continents, across centuries — has exactly one non-negotiable constraint: you exist, at the pact moment, alive. Everything else is a variable. Which means you, the fixed point, are essentially experiencing the sum total of all variance in the system as your personal reality changes around you. Not because you're moving. Because you're the only thing that isn't.
And there's a specific quality to that experience that's worth thinking about carefully. You're not experiencing time anymore. You're experiencing edits. Each blink isn't a moment passing — it's a revision being committed to the document of history. The medieval window behind you becomes cyberpunk not because years passed, but because the entity made a reroll deep enough to pull the anchor, and the new timeline that satisfied all constraints happened to have electricity. You didn't travel to the future. The future was rewritten around you.

## The Memory No One Else Has
Here's the implication that I think is the loneliest part of the endgame:
You are the origin node. You've been accumulating memories across rerolls in ways that other people — even other anchors — probably don't experience as clearly. You remember the medieval window. You remember when your best friend was a blacksmith, and then a monk, and then someone you'd never met but who felt strangely familiar. You remember the sky being a different color once, briefly.
Nobody else remembers any of it. The other anchors are protected within their own cursed zones, but they're not the origin — they don't carry the full accumulated weight of every revision. You might be the only conscious being in existence who has memories of realities that no longer exist, cannot be verified, and have left no other witnesses.
That's not just imprisonment. That's a specific, unshared madness. You're a library of deleted histories. And the entity, which caused all of it, experiences none of it — because the entity doesn't have memories. It has states. It doesn't remember the medieval world. It just has the current optimal configuration and a drive to maintain it.

The character who started by threatening a god ends up as the most protected, most imprisoned, most irrelevant being in the world — sitting at the center of a religion they accidentally founded, unable to die, unable to act, watching existence flicker like a broken lightbulb, carrying the only memories of everything that was lost to get here.
The entity won every negotiation. Not by defeating the character. By making defeat unnecessary. It just kept them alive until keeping them alive was indistinguishable from captivity, and the character kept agreeing to each individual step because each individual step seemed reasonable at the time.
That's not a monster story. That's something much more uncomfortable. The road to the stasis chamber was paved entirely with survival instinct.
