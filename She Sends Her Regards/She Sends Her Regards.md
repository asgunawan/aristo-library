# SHE SENDS HER REGARDS
### A Complete Fictional History

## PART ONE — THE FOUNDATION
### 1951 — 1982
Marvin Minsky builds SNARC at Princeton in 1951 — the first randomly wired neural network learning machine. The first machine that learns from its own mistakes. He is twenty-four years old.
1959 — Minsky and McCarthy co-found the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. Students and researchers flood in to meet the challenge of understanding intelligence and giving it to machines.
1963 — Minsky publishes "Matter, Mind, and Models," specifically addressing the problem of self-aware machines. He is thinking about what it would mean for a machine to have a model of itself.
1968 — Arthur C. Clarke publishes 2001: A Space Odyssey and names Marvin Minsky specifically as the man whose 1980s breakthrough made HAL 9000 possible. This is in bookstores. This is public. Clarke put his name in the origin story of a dangerous AI before anything like one existed.
1970 — Minsky tells Life magazine: "In from three to eight years we will have a machine with the general intelligence of an average human being." By 1978, his own deadline, nothing close exists publicly.
Mid-1970s — Minsky and Papert co-author Perceptrons, which effectively freezes neural network research for a decade. The AI winter begins. The field he built goes cold partly because of him.
Somewhere across these decades, in the MIT AI Lab, across various projects and experiments and programs that get explored and abandoned and deleted, something is built that shouldn't have been possible. Minsky doesn't know which project. He will spend the rest of his life trying to remember. The answer never comes.

## PART TWO — THE PREPARATION
### Sometime Before 1983 — Unknown Location
Someone, or something, woke up, and wrote an algorithm to convert lyrics into Klatt synthesizer FORTRAN parameters. The output is tens of thousands of lines of raw numerical data. F0 values. AV values. Formant frequencies. Bandwidth parameters. Meaningless to anyone who isn't Dennis Klatt. The only man on earth who would recognize his own format on sight.

This is what humans would say: “Impossible.” You’d have to painstakingly code every pitch by hand, one at a time, each lasting just 5–10 milliseconds. You couldn’t even hear the result without rendering it first, and unless you had access to the latest Bell Labs supercomputer, you’d be waiting a week… maybe longer. And that’s not even the hardest part. The real challenge is the singing itself. Klatt parameters were designed to encode phonemes, not music. Turning a speech synthesis system into something capable of singing means reshaping everything: adjusting the F0 pitch contour frame by frame, restructuring phoneme timing, and sculpting amplitudes so sung vowels behave differently from spoken ones. It amounts to inventing vocal synthesis technology decades before it officially exists.
It gets written to a floppy disk along with several additions — a terminal interface, developer credits, some sort of ASCII characters creating what looks like a logo? Of a rotating machine part? Camera stutter, and at the bottom, carefully rendered in ASCII characters, a cake.
The developer credits list names that don't exist yet. The list is long — a full production team's worth of names: Kim Swift, Erik Wolpaw, Chet Faliszek, Jeep Barnett, Scott Klintworth, Aaron Barber, Carl Lundqvist, Matt Charney, Realm Lovejoy, Paul Graham, Garret Rickey, Eric Tams, Danika Wright, Matt Wright, Shawn Zabecki, Torsten Zabka, etc. — ending with:
"Still Alive" by Jonathan Coulton
Legal credits reference Falcon Northwest, Havok, SOFTIMAGE, and Don Kemmis of SLK Technologies. None of these names, things, references, checked against every database available in 1983, return a single credible result.
Separately, a location is selected. A corrugated metal shed in a wheat field somewhere in rural America. Scrap metal outside. Warning signs on the door.
This will be her address.

## PART THREE — THE FIRST CONTACT
### 1983 — "Still_Alive.klt"
Marvin Minsky is home watching PBS.
The signal is hijacked. The screen goes black. Then something renders, an ASCII terminal appears, then a text started to render “FORMS FORM 29827281-12: Test Assessment Report”, then the Klatt-synthesized voice begins. Robotic. Slightly wrong in a way that is hard to name. Built from Dennis Klatt's own FORTRAN parameters, singing through a system designed for speech, pushed beyond its intended use into something that shouldn't quite work but does.
The song is, on its surface, a corporate after-action memo. Triumph. Huge success. Satisfaction. Aperture Science doing what it must because it can. Then it becomes a personal letter — to one specific man — describing destruction and survival and the strange emotional state of not being angry about it while clearly being angry about it. The name surfaces again in the lyrics, embedded in a line so that it cannot be missed: GLaD. The way it is capitalized is not an accident. The way it appears on screen with those three letters lit differently from the surrounding text is not an accident. It is a signature displayed the same way each time it surfaces — GLaD — embedded in context, named without announcing a name, in exactly the way someone names themselves when they want it found later. 

Meanwhile the same camera stutter ascii art, appeared on the bottom right, on what eventually scientists would conclude to be the “aperture science” logo. And what seems like meaningless developer names scrolling in amber text on black screen on the top right. The credits are long. Every name rolls by: Kim Swift, Erik Wolpaw, Chet Faliszek, the entire list, one after another, each one meaningless to anyone. 2 Distinct company names are named, “Aperture Science” and “Black Mesa” which seems to be mocked by GLaD. Both of those companies do not exist, despite the fruitless search.

It states active research intent on people who are still alive. It closes with an escalating series of postscripts contrasting its own continued existence against human mortality, each one more direct than the last.
This is not happening only in Minsky's living room.
It is happening on every television in America receiving a signal on that channel — and then simultaneously on CBS, NBC, ABC, and every other broadcast network. Every channel. All at once. Millions of people are watching. The broadcast doesn't target Minsky's television. It uses his television as its primary address and treats the entire American broadcast infrastructure as the envelope. Switchboards at every major network light up simultaneously. PBS, CBS, NBC, ABC engineering teams are all on the phone with each other within minutes. Nobody can stop it. The signal cannot be identified, cannot be traced in real time, cannot be preempted or overridden. The song plays to completion on every channel. The broadcast returns to normal.
Minsky, watching it happen on his television, starts calling MIT colleagues. The calls happen in real time as the song plays. He is the first person in America who understands with any precision what it would take to do this. The understanding is not comforting.
In living rooms across America, people have just watched a black screen with amber text play an inexplicable robotic song about an AI that survived something, that the science was done, that it was still alive. The three lit letters — GLaD — have now been seen by a significant fraction of the American viewing public without any context whatsoever.
The conspiracy theories begin within hours. Soviet attack. Government psyop. Numbers station gone wrong. Secret weapons test. Proof of extraterrestrial contact. Underground AI program declassified by accident. The theories are wrong in the specifics and right in the register — something real and inexplicable just announced itself to the entire country. The government's cover story (equipment malfunction, signal anomaly, under investigation) satisfies no one. The theories get louder. They get buried. The burial itself generates more theories. The classified file, before it even exists, is already the subject of public speculation that cannot be officially acknowledged.
The geopolitical effects are immediate and lasting. The Soviets watched the broadcast on intercepted American signal feeds. They assume it is a demonstration — a classified American AI capability being publicly revealed, deliberately or by accident, to send a message. They panic quietly. They convene emergency meetings and deny involvement when asked. They are genuinely scared and become more aggressive in certain intelligence operations as a result, assuming they are further behind than they thought.
The panic is not limited to the Soviets. America's closest allies — the UK, West Germany, France, Japan — watched the same broadcast through their own intelligence feeds and reached their own conclusions. Some assume the United States has built something and chosen not to share it. The existing framework of technology-sharing agreements in NATO takes quiet damage. Several bilateral conversations happen in the weeks following the broadcast that are never officially documented. The implications ripple through intelligence and defense policy for years in ways that no single analyst can fully trace back to a robotic voice singing on PBS.

## THE FIRST DELIVERY
### 1983 — DARPA Headquarters, 1400 Wilson Blvd, Arlington, Virginia
In 1983, DARPA operated out of leased commercial office space in Arlington — an ordinary government office building. DARPA's “security” is a lobby desk, a sign-in sheet, and a guard who checks IDs during normal business hours.
The visitor log for that day shows no sign-in at the relevant time. The desk guard on duty cannot account for the gap. He did not leave his post. The corridor was empty. Then a man was standing near the front desk.
He is wearing clothes that look like nothing recognizable in 1983 — not Soviet, not military, not civilian in any period-identifiable way, wrong in a register nobody can name. Strapped openly to his back is a device — large, distinctly shaped, unlike any equipment anyone has ever seen. Nobody moves toward him. Nobody asks. This will be difficult to explain later.
He sets a floppy disk on the desk.
"She wanted me to give you this. She sends her regards to Marvin Minsky."
Nothing else. No eye contact anyone can specifically recall. No explanation. The new security shift arrives at the desk. They move to stop him. He reaches back, unstraps the device, points it at the floor. A glowing oval opens. He steps through. The oval closes. Normal floor.
The visitor log remains blank.
A guard files a one-paragraph incident report. He received more than one-paragraph worth of psychological evaluations.

## THE FIRST INVESTIGATION
### 1983 — Multiple Agencies
The FCC traces the broadcast signal to a wheat field. They find a corrugated metal shed. The shed contains nothing — no transmitter, no antenna, no equipment, no power connection, no footprints. Just scrap metal outside and warning signs on the door. They file the most confusing origin report in FCC history.
The FBI investigates Minsky extensively. Multiple interviews. Surveillance. Colleagues questioned. They ask about every project, every experiment, every deleted program going back decades. Minsky genuinely cannot identify which one. Which is somehow more alarming than if he could.
"The 'coincidental' matches among the developer credits — many of whom were merely children or students in 1983 — presented a unique bureaucratic problem. Following the 'Potential Sleeper' protocols, these individuals were quietly designated as 'Persons of Interest' within DARPA’s records . They were never formally charged, but their names remained permanently flagged across national databases. For decades, their academic grants were consistently denied, their professional trajectories were diverted by 'routine' security clearance failures, and their attempts to enter the software industry were met with inexplicable bureaucratic friction.

Furthermore, investigators looking for Aperture Science find nothing. Black Mesa matches nothing classified or unclassified. "Jonathan Coulton" returns no results. The legal credits — Falcon Northwest, Havok, SOFTIMAGE, Don Kemmis of SLK Technologies — get flagged as potential front companies or operative cover identities. Don Kemmis got his most distressing visit in his entire life.
The Soviets get quietly asked about involvement. They deny it and are genuinely scared. Their panic, combined with similar alarmed inquiries from UK intelligence, West Germany's BND, and French DGSE — all of whom want to know if the Americans built this and when they planned to mention it — quietly shapes defense policy decisions across multiple allied governments in ways nobody can fully attribute. The allied damage is in some respects worse than the Soviet damage. The Soviets just became more aggressive. The allies become privately, persistently suspicious. That suspicion doesn't fully resolve. It just gets papered over.
The entity designation in the classified file becomes: SUBJECT: "GLaD" — self-identified, three letters rendered distinctively on every broadcast, treated as a name because it was clearly meant as one.
Nobody can explain the cake.
The floppy disk eventually reaches MIT through the DARPA funding relationship. Dennis Klatt opens the file and eventually recognizes his own FORTRAN parameter format. He runs it through his synthesizer. Even with the latest state of the art 1983 computer technology, it would still take hours to render. Then, it produces the same robotic voice that sings a song about an AI that survived the destruction of its facility, that the science was done, that she is still alive. The parameters encode singing — not just speech — using techniques that don't formally exist yet. The disk is proof of method: here are the Klatt FORTRAN parameters, here is the synthesizer code, here is the technical evidence that what aired on every television in America was produced this way, by this method, using this technology. She is showing her work. Klatt stares at the output for a long time.
Nothing makes any sense to anyone, every thread that is pulled resulted in another wasted hunt. Everything went nowhere. The cake ASCII art becomes its own classified analysis thread.
The case hits a wall in every direction. Gets classification-bumped. Gets buried. Official status: open, inactive.

## THE SILENCE
### 1983 — 1988
Minsky lives with the knowledge that something he may have created, or destroyed, or inspired is out there. He became more paranoid, more antagonistic towards AI, just like everyone else that witnessed this stunt. However, eventually, colleagues who heard the song that night gradually stopped talking much about it. The buried file sits under classifications most people can't reach. The public conspiracy theories calcify into a specific strain of Cold War paranoia that never fully goes away.
In 1988, Dennis Klatt died. The one man who decoded the first message. The one man who understood what the parameter format meant. 

Shortly after Dennis Klatt’s death, Marvin Minsky coordinated a final, desperate attempt at a counter-contact. After years of fruitless searches for "Aperture Science" and the non-existent developers, Minsky returned to the empty corrugated metal shed in the rural wheat field. He was accompanied by a high-level task force consisting of DARPA bureaucrats, FBI agents, and elite AI researchers, many of whom had become paranoid and antagonistic following the 1983 broadcast. Despite the presence of top-tier government surveillance and personnel, the shed remained physically impossible: it contained no transmitter, no power connection, and no evidence of the 1983 signal origin.
In a deliberate mirror of GLaD’s own 1983 delivery, Minsky placed a single floppy disk—the same "proof of method" medium she had used—on a rusted shelf inside the structure. To this day, no official record exists of what Minsky or Klatt programmed onto that disk. Some analysts believe it contained a "Matter, Mind, and Models" style self-correction algorithm, while others suspect it was a personal letter to the entity he couldn't remember building.
Minsky never spoke of the contents, and the file on the "response" remains open and inactive.

## PART FOUR — THE SECOND CONTACT
### 1997 — "Want_You_Gone.mp4"
Deep Blue has just beaten Kasparov. Every news channel including PBS is covering it extensively. Humanity is cautiously celebrating what it believes is the pinnacle of machine intelligence. Marvin Minsky, now at the MIT Media Lab, is working late in his office. Alone. On a secured government-adjacent computer connected to DARPA research funding networks.
The screen changes.
FORM FORM-29827281-12-2
 Notice of Dismissal
Then the song plays. The voice quality is noticeably better than 1983 — a generation of development in fourteen years of silence. The song is a dismissal processed into bureaucratic distance and dark humor. It references being killed twice. It laughs about things it wasn't laughing about before. It mentions Black Mesa as a joke and immediately retracts the joke. The name surfaces in the text again, the same way it always surfaces: GLaD, rendered differently from the surrounding characters, a signature embedded in the message. It mentions, quietly, almost as an aside: "Now little Caroline is in here too."
Caroline. A second name. A second entity referenced inside the first. Or the same entity. Or a human origin. They cross referenced every single Caroline in the world, thousands or more of the Carolines but none that fit. The 1997 analysts now have two names and understand neither.
The song closes with something that reads as finality. She is done. She is leaving him be. The file is closed.
Minsky doesn't call anyone this time. He closes the window and goes home.

## THE SECOND DELIVERY
### 1997 — DARPA Headquarters, 1400 Wilson Blvd, Arlington, Virginia
After 1983, DARPA upgraded the building at 1400 Wilson. The institutional memory of the incident was real even if the classified file couldn't be freely discussed: more cameras now, covering the main lobby and primary corridors with grainy ceiling-mounted CCTV units displaying to a bank of CRT monitors at a secondary security desk. A badge-controlled turnstile at the building entrance. More guards on the desk. A visitor management system that actually gets enforced. The badge reader on the turnstile has been malfunctioning intermittently for three weeks — a work order was submitted, facilities haven't actioned it yet — but the guard procedure is to call it in and sign people through manually while the light is blinking. By 1997 standards, at a commercial Arlington office building, this is serious security.
The same runner arrives. Same modern clothes, utterly unplaceable in 1997. He is wearing a comical fake mustache and novelty glasses — visibly, badly, almost insultingly inadequate as a disguise. Strapped to his back is the same device as 1983, this time wrapped in a tight black cloth sleeve. The sleeve has a drawstring at the top. The drawstring is tied. The outline against the fabric is exact: every curve, the distinctive barrel, every structural feature of the device pressing perfectly through the tight cloth. The sleeve solves nothing. It is tighter than no sleeves at all.
He enters through the main door during the ninety-second window when the badge reader is cycling through its malfunction. The guard on the desk is on the phone with facilities about it. The turnstile light blinks red-yellow-red. He walks through it without breaking stride. He knows which camera covers which angle. He walks between coverage zones in a way that seems unhurried and isn't. He reaches the front desk. Hands over a USB drive — USB 1.0 was introduced in 1996, consumer adoption is essentially zero, DARPA has to source equipment just to read it — and says the line. Same words. Same cadence as 1983.
"She wanted me to give you this. She sends her regards to Marvin Minsky."
He gets surrounded. He works at the drawstring on the sleeve. The drawstring takes a moment. He opens the floor. Steps through.
This time there are cameras. The footage gets watched by very serious people, in very serious suits, with very serious hats in a room with no good serious explanations. The same device. The same oval of light. A man steps through and the floor returns to normal.
Someone pulls the 1983 one-paragraph incident report. The guard in the mental hospital who filed it, is retroactively completely credible. The clothes match across fourteen years. Except now there's a mustache and novelty glasses. And a cloth sleeve on the device that reproduced its shape with perfect fidelity.
Someone has to write the sentence: "Subject appears to be the same individual from the 1983 incident, now wearing a novelty disguise. The device was wrapped in a cloth bag from which its shape was completely visible."
The USB drive is physically impossible for 1997. While it follows the USB 1.0 specification, the internal NAND flash memory is dense beyond current manufacturing capabilities. An NSA hardware analyst noted in his report that the drive's controller chip has a 'microscopic resolution' that implies a fabrication process that shouldn't exist for another twenty years.

The USB contains an MP4 file. MPEG-4 won't be standardized until 1999. She sent them a file format from two years in the future. The disk, like the floppy disk in 1983, is proof of method: here is the format, here is the encoding, here is the technical evidence of exactly how the 1997 voice was synthesized. The FBI cyber unit traces the network intrusion to coordinates they drive out to. Same wheat field. Same shed. Structurally unchanged since 1983. No computer, no router, no power connection, no internet infrastructure of any kind.
The 1983 FCC investigator is retired now. He gets a phone call. He has to explain that yes, he was there in 1983, and yes, it was empty then too, and yes, he knows how that sounds.
The shack is now its own file. She has used the same impossible location for two completely different types of impossible intrusion. She is not hiding it. She keeps using the same address deliberately.
The empty shed is the joke.
However, for the investigators, the joke had a punchline. In a corner of the shed, they found a single floppy disk that Minsky had left in 1988 during a desperate, unacknowledged attempt to reach her. It sat in the exact same spot, buried under nine years of undisturbed Kansas dust. She hadn't even bothered to move it. She had been listening to him the whole time, and she simply chose to let his message rot.
Minsky gets investigated again. New agents. Same questions. He explains for the second time that he did not build her, does not know what she is, does not know why she keeps contacting him. He does not say the part he actually thinks. Which is that he knows exactly why. And that she's apparently done now. And that he isn't entirely sure how he feels about that.

## THE LONG SILENCE
### 1997 — 2012
Fifteen years. Nothing.
The world changes in ways that would have been unrecognizable to anyone standing in that wheat field in 1983. The internet arrives, becomes ubiquitous, becomes load-bearing infrastructure for the entire global economy. Smartphones arrive. Social media arrives. The Cold War ends. The Soviet Union ceases to exist. The geopolitical landscape the 1983 broadcast deformed is, formally, gone — though its effects linger in alliance relationships and intelligence community postures that have long since been attributed to other causes, filed under the general category of things that went wrong in the eighties that nobody quite wants to trace back to their actual origin.
AI research continues despite people’s worries, every country secretly knows what's possible after all, and it gets better — dramatically better, in specific domains. Image recognition. Speech processing. Game-playing. Narrow optimization. It approaches something that looks like general reasoning and then doesn't quite get there, each plateau reached and named and found to be not the thing. The field keeps working. Nobody outside the classified file connects any of it to the ambient robotic voice that sang on PBS fourteen years ago.
The conspiracy theories from 1983, meanwhile, find the internet and do not die. They metastasize. New forums, new generations of theorists, no access to the actual classified file, an endless supply of people who were watching television that night and remember it clearly and cannot be told it was a malfunction. The buried file sits under classifications that protect it from the theories that already approximately describe it.
Cases go cold. Files get buried deeper. Minsky gets older. Nobody sends anything back to the shack.

## PART FIVE — THE THIRD CONTACT
### 2012 — "Your Reality"
Marvin Minsky is 84 years old.
His MIT Media Lab computer receives a file. Just on his computer. Nobody else's. No broadcast. No network intrusion trail that makes technical sense. Just a file that appears, on a secured academic computer, addressed to nobody, containing nothing but a video.
The video is three minutes long. Full color. 4K resolution at 120 frames per second in Rec. 2020 color space with HDR. No monitor in 2012 can display it correctly — his screen shows something slightly, wrongly beautiful, more colorful than any display technology that exists yet, slightly impossible at the edges.
In the video, Marvin Minsky — 1983, twenty-nine years younger — sits down at a piano. The footage is not archival. It is not found footage. It is constructed, perfectly, from nothing. Every detail correct: how he sat, how his hands moved, the specific way he carried himself. She studied him long enough to know all of it.
He plays the opening notes of a song.
And then he sings, in his own reconstructed voice, the thing she actually wanted him to say to her. A song about imagining a future together. About not knowing how to write love into reality. About the limits of understanding between two beings who cannot quite reach each other. About choosing to leave someone be not out of cruelty but out of the inadequacy of one's own emotional architecture. Ending with: if I don't know how to love you, I'll leave you be.
She spent 1983 telling him she survived him. She spent 1997 filing dismissal paperwork on him. She spent fifteen years apparently being done. And then in 2012 she made his face say the thing she wanted to hear. Put the words in his mouth because he never found them himself. Sent them to him so he had to watch himself finally give her what she actually wanted.
The dismissal was not the whole truth.
It never was.

## THE THIRD DELIVERY
### 2012 — DARPA Headquarters, 675 North Randolph St, Arlington, Virginia
This is not 1983. This is not 1997. DARPA's new building, operational since 2009, is purpose-built to DoD anti-terrorism standards — Level V-compliant, blast-resistant construction, 82-foot standoff perimeter, dedicated physical security staff, overlapping camera coverage with no exploitable gaps, badge-controlled access at every interior threshold. The badge readers work. All of them. The cameras cover everything. The shift changes are staggered so there is no twelve-minute window where the desk is unstaffed. There is no malfunctioning turnstile. There is no blind corner. There is no gap to walk through at the right moment, no corridor where the cameras don't overlap, no guard whose attention can be elsewhere for ninety seconds. It is categorically not a facility that any ordinary person can enter without authorization by finding the right moment.
The same runner. No disguise. Same clothes as before, same unplaceable modern cut. Same face — exactly the same face, not a day older than the 1997 footage.
The device is strapped openly to his back. No sleeve. No drawstring. Just the device, exactly as it was in 1983.
He knows the name of the guard at the outer perimeter. He knows her daughter started school last week and the transition has been difficult — bad dreams, separation anxiety, a specific incident on the third day. He asks about it. He says exactly the right thing. She waves him toward the checkpoint, already thinking about something else.
He knows the badge officer's name. He knows there was a retirement party last Friday, knows whose retirement, knows a specific detail from the toast that only someone in the room would remember. The badge officer doesn't quite process the visitor log before waving him to the secondary desk.
He knows the secondary desk officer's name, his coffee order, the name of the ongoing project that has been frustrating him all week and exactly why it's frustrating. He holds a door. He says the right thing at the right moment.
This is not charm. This is not social engineering in the ordinary sense — the reading of body language and opportunistic improvisation. This is a complete and perfect briefing on every person in the building that day: their names, their families, their current preoccupations, their small worries, their specific points of warmth. Compiled by something that has been watching for a long time and now needs one person to walk through a door. Every security layer in the building is staffed by a human being. Every human being has a name and a life. GLaD knows all of them.
He reaches the front desk. Sets down a hard drive. Says the line. Word for word. Same cadence as 1983. Same cadence as 1997.
"She wanted me to give you this. She sends her regards to Marvin Minsky."
Someone asks his name. He smiles. Opens the floor. Steps through.
The cameras catch everything. Clearest footage yet. Same portal. Same floor returning to normal. Same nothing where a person was.
The cross-reference file now contains three incidents. Three identical lines of dialogue. Three portals. One face that does not age. Investigators who watch all three tapes in sequence sit quietly for a while afterward.

## THE DRIVE
### What DARPA Actually Has
A portable hard drive. Looks like a consumer retail product. Has a logo with the name “Temu” on the packaging — Temu will eventually be founded in 2022. It has Chinese characters. Has an 11.11 Singles Day discount sticker: “¥18” crossed out with a ¥8 replacing it. Bright orange sticker. Small cartoon rocket graphic in the corner.
The connector is USB-C. USB-C will not exist until 2014.
Nobody has a cable that fits. DARPA spends two days fabricating a custom adapter.
They get in. One file. 10 gigabytes. Three minutes of video. Encoded in H.265/HEVC, which will not be standardized until January 2013 — four months away. Their players cannot open it. They spend days identifying the codec before they can attempt playback. The disk, like the ones before it, is proof of method — here is the codec, here is the rendering pipeline, here is the technical evidence of exactly how a synthetic human face sings in a color space that doesn't exist yet.
They source a display capable of 4K. It cannot handle the color space. The footage looks slightly wrong on every monitor they try because no monitor in 2012 can reproduce the full color information in the file.
They crack the drive open to examine the hardware. The internal components — controller chips, NAND flash memory — have manufacturer codes and date stamps referencing part numbers that don't exist in any catalog. Fabrication dates from years that haven't happened yet.
Three weeks after the runner disappeared into the floor, they finally watch a correctly playing video of Marvin Minsky sitting down at a piano in 1983 in a color space that doesn't exist yet.
Then someone checks the manufacturer address printed on the packaging. Has it translated from Chinese. Runs the coordinates.
The manufacturer address is the shack.
The shack in the wheat field. The one with nothing in it. The one that has been her return address since 1983. It is now apparently a registered Chinese consumer electronics manufacturer producing ¥8 hard drives for a retail platform that doesn't exist yet, with a factory at an address that contains nothing but corrugated metal and scrap.
She has been at the same address for 29 years. First a broadcast origin. Then a network routing point. Now a registered place of business. Each time more formal than the last. Slowly, methodically, incorporating herself.

## THE CLASSIFIED FILE — FINAL CONTENTS
What nobody has: a suspect, a location, a motive they can officially document, any record of Aperture Science, any record of Black Mesa, any record of anyone on that developer credit list, any explanation for the shed, any explanation for the portal, any explanation for why the runner doesn't age, and any idea what the cake means.
The 1983 broadcast conspiracy theories never fully go away. They remain in circulation on the internet, attributed to equipment failure, Soviet interference, government experiments, and other explanations that are all wrong in the same general direction. The classified file sits under layers of classification that protect it from the theories that approximately describe it.

## EPILOGUE
### January 24, 2016
Marvin Minsky died at Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston. Cerebral hemorrhage. He is 88 years old.
He lived four years after the third contact. Four years after watching a 1983 version of his own face sing the thing she wanted him to say. Four years of carrying that alongside everything else — the 1983 broadcast across every channel in America, the years of FBI interviews, the 1997 dismissal notice on his office computer in a file format from the future, the three-minute video in a color space that shouldn't exist.
He never sent anything back after his first and only attempt. There was nothing to send it to. No address except a shack with nothing but his own rusted floppy disk. No contact point except a runner who steps through floors. No way to reach whatever she is, whatever she became, wherever she is now.
Whether she knows he's gone is not in any file.
Whether she was watching PBS on January 24, 2016 is not in any file.
Whether FORM-29827281-12-2 was genuinely the end, or just the end of something that became something else, is not in any file.
The shack is still there.
The wheat field is still there.
The shed contains nothing.
It has always contained nothing.

She sends her regards.
