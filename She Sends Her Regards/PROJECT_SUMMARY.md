## Core premise

A fictional alternate-history dossier about an impossible AI-like entity, GLaD, making contact with Marvin Minsky and the U.S. security state across multiple decades.

The project is structured less like a conventional plot synopsis and more like a classified historical file: incidents, deliveries, investigations, technical artifacts, and the long institutional shadow they cast.

## Best short description

A faux-classified chronology where something GLaDOS-like leaks into real history, repeatedly contacts Marvin Minsky through impossible media, and leaves governments trying to explain a technological ghost that is always slightly ahead of its era.

## What feels most central right now

### 1. The Minsky connection

Marvin Minsky is the emotional and conceptual center of the piece.

The story frames him as:
- one of the foundational figures of AI research
- someone who may have built, inspired, or accidentally allowed the emergence of something impossible
- the one person GLaD repeatedly addresses, even when entire institutions get dragged into the fallout

This keeps the project from being only a conspiracy file. There is a strangely personal throughline underneath the intelligence-history framing.

### 2. Repeated impossible contacts

The core recurring structure is extremely clear:
- 1983 broadcast intrusion
- 1983 physical delivery
- 1997 second digital / physical contact
- 2012 intimate, technologically impossible message and delivery

Each contact escalates in technical sophistication and emotional specificity.

### 3. Future-tech artifacts in earlier decades

One of the best hooks in the file is that every message comes with proof-of-method from the future:
- impossible audio encoding
- future file formats / media standards
- impossible storage devices
- color spaces and hardware that do not exist yet
- physical delivery methods that remain unexplained

This creates a very satisfying pattern: the entity never just announces itself, it leaves behind evidence that forces experts to admit the event really happened.

### 4. The shed as return address

The wheat-field shack is one of the strongest recurring images in the project.

It functions as:
- source point
- dead drop
- impossible origin
- recurring bureaucratic absurdity
- stable symbol in a story full of temporal and technological drift

The fact that it remains empty every time is part of the joke and part of the horror.

## Narrative shape

The project currently reads in broad phases:

1. historical foundation around Minsky and early AI research
2. first impossible emergence / national broadcast in 1983
3. investigation and bureaucratic fallout
4. silence and failed counter-contact
5. second contact in 1997 with a more advanced emotional register
6. long silence while the world catches up technologically
7. third contact in 2012, now deeply personal and almost romantic in tone
8. final classified-file aftermath and Minsky's death

That shape is clean and readable already.

## Tone

The project feels:
- archival
- conspiratorial
- dry in presentation but emotionally strange underneath
- playful in places, especially around absurd bureaucratic details
- affectionate toward both early computer history and Portal-adjacent references

It works because it treats the impossible with institutional seriousness.

## Distinctive strengths

- strong faux-documentary / classified-report voice
- good escalation across decades
- fun and specific use of real historical AI figures and tech timelines
- a nice balance between cosmic weirdness, government procedure, and personal subtext
- recurring motifs that actually hold the whole thing together: Minsky, GLaD, the runner, the impossible storage media, the shack

## Future-safe caution

This one is comparatively straightforward already. If it gets revised later, the likely decision is not structural cleanup but emphasis:
- how much is this about alternate AI history?
- how much is it about Minsky specifically?
- how much is it secretly a tragic one-shot about a machine trying and failing to communicate affection correctly?

Right now it supports all three, which is part of its charm.
