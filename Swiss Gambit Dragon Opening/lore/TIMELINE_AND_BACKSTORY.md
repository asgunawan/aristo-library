# Swiss Timeline And Identity Recommendation

This note answers the current cluster of questions together:

- Is 2 years enough?
- How old should he be?
- Should he be a student, programmer, or something else before transfer?
- What is the least-wrong baseline that keeps the story coherent?

## Short Answer

The strongest current version is probably:

- age: 20 when he returns
- pre-transfer: second-year broad practical student
- fantasy-world absence: about 2 years
- Earth-side anchor: light but real, not orphaned and not heavily rooted

That is the cleanest balance.

## Why 2 Years Is Actually Good

I think 2 years is close to the sweet spot.

It is long enough that:
- he can be genuinely transformed
- he can form real bonds in the fantasy kingdom
- he can plausibly take part in a demon-lord-scale crisis
- his old Earth life no longer fits cleanly

But it is short enough that:
- he is still recognizably young
- returning to school or civilian life is not absurd on paper
- the missing-person angle still works
- he has not fully calcified into a fantasy-world adult ruler

That last part matters a lot.

If you make it 4 years or more, then several things start changing:
- Earth life becomes harder to plausibly resume in any form
- the fantasy world starts looking like his obvious primary home
- his political maturity may feel too settled
- the awkwardness of Arc 1 gets weaker because he is less half-formed

So for this specific story, longer is not automatically better.

## Why 16 Is Too Young

I would avoid making him 16.

That creates problems you probably do not want:
- the fantasy-world authority gets harder to buy
- Earth-side legal issues become even more intense
- the story gets pulled toward child-vulnerability dynamics
- political scenes can start feeling distorted by age alone

Also, the specific tone you seem to want is not "kid hero thrown into geopolitics."

It is more like:
- young adult
- underestimated
- not fully formed
- old enough to be taken seriously once he speaks well

That points more toward 19 to 21, not 16.

## Why 20 Works Better Than 18

18 can work, but 20 is probably easier.

At 20, he can still be:
- immature enough to feel strange in diplomacy
- young enough to have a broken student life
- easy to underestimate

But also:
- old enough that fantasy-side leadership does not feel quite as silly
- old enough to plausibly have some independent housing, money issues, and administrative freedom
- old enough that a 2-year transformation feels substantial rather than impossible

So I think 20-on-return is the best target.

That implies roughly:
- transferred around 18
- returns around 20

Which is very usable.

## What He Was Before Transfer

The exact label matters less than the function.

He should be someone who:
- is smart but not specialized to the point of clichÃ©
- knows enough about modern systems to notice what the kingdom lacks
- can look things up and synthesize quickly
- is not already a finished adult professional

That is why broad practical student still seems strongest.

## Best Major Fit

Because you are a programmer and want something you can write comfortably, I would not throw away that familiarity entirely.

The best compromise is probably:

### information systems

Why this is probably the best fit:
- it keeps enough tech familiarity that you can write him naturally
- it is broader and less stereotyped than pure CS genius protagonist
- it supports systems thinking, workflows, data, logistics, and problem translation
- it fits a character who becomes a bridge rather than an inventor-god

If you want a second option, use:

### supply chain / logistics

That is also very strong, but it may be a little less intuitive for you to write from the inside if your natural language is closer to tech.

So my recommendation is:

### an information-systems student with broad practical instincts and some logistics-minded habits.

That gets you most of the benefits without locking him into "hacker chosen one" nonsense.

## Orphan Versus Anchor

I do not think full orphan is the best choice anymore.

It solves a tactical problem, but weakens the emotional shape.

The healthier version is:

### one light but real Earth anchor

For example:
- an aunt who helped raise him
- an older sister he is not constantly close to but does care about
- one parent with a somewhat strained but real relationship

This gives you enough that:
- his disappearance matters
- his return matters
- Earth feels like a place of human ties, not just infrastructure

But not so much that every scene becomes family-hostage realism.

## What The 2 Years Probably Look Like

If you want the cleanest version, the two fantasy years probably should not mean he single-handedly solved an empire from scratch.

Better:

### Year 1
He survives, becomes useful, earns trust, and helps turn a catastrophic losing position into a survivable one.

### Year 2
He becomes politically important because he helped coordinate the kingdom's survival, helped repel the demon lord threat, and became tied to the postwar stabilization problem.

That is more believable than "teen student fully became legendary emperor in 24 months."

He should return as:
- war-tested
- respected
- maybe granted office or authority

But still emotionally unfinished.

That is the sweet spot.

## Why He Still Prefers Earth

Yes, it is very justifiable that he would rather live on Earth.

In fact, that is one of the story's strengths.

He can love the kingdom and still prefer Earth because Earth offers:
- stable medicine
- working infrastructure
- normal entertainment
- privacy
- lower daily stakes
- the right to be ordinary

That is extremely believable.

The fantasy world matters because of bonds and obligation.
Earth matters because it is where life is allowed to be normal.

That is not a contradiction.
That is the whole tension.

## Best Arc 1 Shape

Your instinct here is probably correct too.

Arc 1 should likely be:
- re-adaptation attempt
- double life strain
- some gold-selling and procurement improvisation
- increasing intelligence attention
- repeated fantasy-world pullbacks
- slow realization that normal student life cannot be sustained
- Swiss recognition as the end-of-arc break

That gives the story a clear tonal shift:

### first half
awkward, tense, almost darkly funny double life

### end of arc
he chooses diplomacy and statehood on purpose

That is much stronger than starting with full formal diplomacy immediately.

## My Recommended Baseline

If I had to lock a neutral version right now, I would use:

### He is an 18-year-old information-systems student when summoned, lightly tethered to Earth through one real adult relative and a few ordinary social ties. He returns at 20 after roughly two fantasy years, having helped a kingdom survive and repel a demon-lord-scale crisis, but not having fully grown into the political role he was forced to occupy there.

That version gives you:
- enough transformation
- enough awkwardness
- enough Earth pull
- enough fantasy legitimacy
- enough room for Arc 1 to actually be about him

## Short Version

Keep him around 20 on return, make the absence about 2 years, avoid making him 16 or a fully settled older adult, and use a broad practical student identity such as information systems. That gives you the best balance between Earth awkwardness, fantasy credibility, and the protagonist-centered double-life story you seem to want.

# Swiss Pre-Transfer Backstory Options

This note answers the current question more directly:

Ignore the existing draft noise for a moment.
What should the protagonist's Earth life look like before he got transferred?

Because this choice controls a lot:
- how much he misses modern life
- how much Earth can still emotionally matter to him
- how easy he is to underestimate
- how easy he is to control
- whether returning to normal life feels possible, impossible, or tragic

## The Main Problem

You are correctly noticing a real tension.

### If he is a total orphan with no anchor

Then:
- it is easier for him to disappear and return
- hostage pressure is lower
- but Earth risks feeling too disposable
- his desire to go back to ordinary modern life gets weaker

### If he has a very strong family anchor

Then:
- his return to Earth becomes emotionally strong
- but the system can grab him much faster
- his disappearance has bigger consequences
- it may become too hard to justify him not simply staying put or being immediately cornered

So the best answer is probably not either extreme.

## What He Actually Needs

He needs enough Earth-anchor to prefer modern life,
but not so much Earth-anchor that the story becomes mostly about family hostage realism.

That means he probably needs:
- familiarity with ordinary modern comfort
- one or two human ties
- an unfinished life rather than a deeply rooted one
- enough autonomy that he can move around and improvise

That is why the student version is still strong.

## The Best Backstory Shape

I think the healthiest baseline is:

### not orphan,
### not deeply rooted,
### lightly tethered.

Meaning:
- he is young
- he has a small personal world
- people care if he vanishes
- but he is not so socially embedded that the story becomes impossible

## Good Earth-Life Models

## Option 1: The Light-Anchor Student

This is my recommended version.

Before transfer, he is:
- a second-year college student or recent dropout-age student
- living near campus, maybe in shared housing or cheap off-campus housing
- not academically brilliant, but curious and competent
- drifting a little, not fully sure what he wants

His Earth ties are:
- one surviving parent, aunt, or older sister he is not perfectly close to but does care about
- two or three friends, more casual than lifelong soul-bonds
- a normal modern routine he took for granted

Why it works:
- people worry when he disappears
- he can return to a believable but not overwhelming life
- Earth still feels emotionally real
- he is not so anchored that every scene becomes family melodrama
- agents can pressure him, but he is not instantly immobilized by five dependent relatives

This is probably the best balance.

## Option 2: The Soft-Orphan Student

This is closer to your current draft, but improved.

Before transfer, he is:
- effectively alone in practical terms
- maybe both parents dead, or one dead and one absent
- financially strained but not destitute
- used to taking care of himself

His Earth anchor is not family.
It is lifestyle.

He misses:
- privacy
- internet
- stable laws
- food variety
- medicine
- boredom
- the right to be unimportant

Why it works:
- easy to move around
- easy to believe he can return quietly for a while
- his preference for modern life makes sense through systems rather than relationships

Weakness:
- can make him feel slightly too untethered emotionally
- increases the temptation to have him simply choose one world in a cleaner way

This can work, but you need to compensate by making his craving for ordinary modern life very vivid.

## Option 3: The Older Professional

Before transfer, he is:
- mid-20s or later
- in a low-status but stable technical or logistics job
- already living alone
- more practiced at adult life

Why it works:
- easier to justify resourcefulness
- easier to write buying, bargaining, and planning

Weakness:
- loses the underestimation advantage
- loses some awkwardness
- makes him feel a bit too mature for the specific double-life tension you seem to like

I do not think this is your best lane.

## What Should Matter More Than "Programmer"

I do not think the exact pre-transfer job or major is the key.

More important is what kind of person he was.

He should probably be:
- curious across domains
- good at learning enough to act
- decent at improvising with incomplete knowledge
- used to searching systems rather than mastering one discipline deeply
- not physically impressive before transfer
- not politically experienced before transfer

That lets the fantasy-world experience actually change him.

## Recommended Neutral-Ground Version

If I had to propose one clean baseline from scratch, it would be this:

### A 20-year-old second-year student from the U.S. Midwest or similar, living a fairly small life, with one surviving adult relative and a few ordinary social ties, studying something broad and practical enough to support systems thinking without locking him into a clichÃ© technical identity.

That broad-and-practical field could be:
- information systems
- supply chain or logistics
- engineering undeclared / general engineering
- economics with technical electives
- environmental engineering early track

Why this is strong:
- he knows just enough about the modern world to know what is useful
- he is not so specialized that he feels fake when he starts grabbing random practical knowledge
- he has enough Earth life to miss it
- he has enough incompleteness that the fantasy world can plausibly transform him fast

## Why Modern Life Should Still Win Emotionally

You asked whether it is justifiable that he would rather live in the modern world even after bonds form in fantasy.

Yes, very easily.

Because medieval fantasy actually does suck in many ordinary ways.

He can love the people there and still prefer Earth for:
- medicine
- toilets
- privacy
- food stability
- entertainment
- legal predictability
- not being needed every hour
- not watching people die from logistics failure

That is not cowardice.
That is sanity.

This is actually a strength of the project.

He is not choosing Earth because he does not care.
He is choosing Earth because it is a place where a person is allowed to stop being a constant emergency instrument.

That is strong character logic.

## Why He Still Goes Back

He goes back because the fantasy world stopped being abstract.

He now has:
- friends
- obligations
- guilt
- unfinished promises
- a kingdom whose survival is partially tied to him

So his tension is not:
- which world is cooler?

It is:
- can he keep the world he prefers from abandoning the world he cannot morally leave behind?

That is the real story.

## My Recommendation On Family Anchor

I would avoid both extremes.

Do not make him fully orphan unless you really want the cleaner tactical version.

Do not give him a huge close family unless you want much more domestic realism pressure.

Best answer:

### one light but real anchor.

Examples:
- an older sister he messages irregularly
- an aunt who helped raise him
- a single mother he loves but is a bit emotionally distant from

That is enough to make his disappearance and return matter.

It is not enough to trap the story.

## My Recommendation On Arc 1

Your instinct here also seems good.

Arc 1 probably should be:
- double-life strain
- re-adaptation failure
- procurement improvisation
- suspicion from agencies
- fantasy-world pull getting stronger
- Swiss recognition as the finale move, not the opening frame

That gives the Swiss move real weight.

It becomes the moment he accepts:
- he cannot go back to being only a student
- he needs a state-level solution
- he must become the bridge on purpose

That is much stronger than starting with Switzerland immediately.

## My Blunt Recommendation

If you want a neutral starting point, use this:

### He was a lightly tethered college student with one real adult anchor, an unfinished ordinary life, broad systems-minded curiosity, and no expectation of becoming important. He returns from two years in fantasy still preferring modern life, but unable to morally detach from the people and obligations he left behind.

That version gives you the most room.

## Short Version

The protagonist probably should not be a full orphan or a heavily rooted family guy. The strongest neutral-ground version is a lightly tethered student with one real Earth anchor and an unfinished ordinary life, because that preserves both halves of the story: Earth still matters enough to miss, and the fantasy world still matters enough that he cannot walk away.
