# Chapter 1

The air was wrong.

That was the first thing Colin noticed, walking down a road he didn't recognize at an hour when no one should be walking. The air had no weight to it. For two years he'd breathed air that pressed back against him, warm and dense and faintly alive, and now he kept taking breaths that came back empty, like sipping at a glass that was never filled.

His clothes were the second thing. They'd been torn, and then mended by hand, and then torn again in different places, so that the repairs and the damage had stopped being separate things. The stitching on his sleeve was kingdom work—careful, even, done by someone who'd learned to fix armor, not shirts. It was the only clean part of him.

He had no bag. No phone. No wallet. He'd left all of it buried in the trees behind him, where he'd come to, maybe twenty minutes ago, sprawled in wet leaves with the taste of ozone in his mouth and the name of a place he no longer knew how to get back to.

He stopped walking and stood in the glow of a gas station sign, reading it. SUNOCO. The word meant something. It meant he was home, or close to it, or in the country that used to be home, which was a different thing and possibly the same thing and he was too tired to sort out which.

A woman inside the station, behind the counter, was watching him through the glass. She'd already got her phone out—holding it up, either filming or pretending not to. She didn't help. She watched him the way you watch a thing that doesn't fit, and she wanted proof it was real.

He raised a hand—not to wave, just to show he was solid—and she flinched behind the phone, and he lowered it again.

Okay. That was fair. He probably looked like the kind of man you called someone about.

He kept walking.

---

The cruiser was parked at the edge of the lot, engine idling, lights off. Colin came toward it at a normal pace, trying to look like a person who belonged to the world he was in, which was harder than it should have been because his body kept doing things he didn't ask it to do. His right hand kept drifting to his hip. Not for a phone. For something that hung there for two years and wasn't there now.

He made himself stop at the car. One hand raised, palm open.

The window came down. An officer looked out—middle-aged, tired in the specific way of a man who'd been parked in a gas station lot since eleven. He took Colin in the same way the woman had: clothes, hour, the way he was standing.

"Long night?" the officer said.

It took Colin a second to find the register. Small talk. He'd been good at this once. Two years of talking to kings and wounded knights and a mage who would only communicate through progressively more detailed diagrams had scraped it out of him.

"Yeah," he said. And then, because he was standing under a gas station sign in Ohio at three in the morning in hand-mended clothes with nothing in his pockets, he asked the only question that mattered to him:

"What year is it?"

The officer stared.

It was a long stare. The kind that did math. The kind that moved Colin from *kid out late* to *something's wrong here* without either of them saying it.

Then the officer told him, and Colin did the arithmetic the way his brain still did everything—automatically, in the background—and came up with the only number that mattered.

Two years. He'd been gone two years. That number was supposed to feel enormous, and instead it just felt like a number, because two years in the kingdom had been a lifetime and a blink, and he hadn't figured out yet which one he'd lost.

"Okay," he said. "Good. That's—good."

"You want to tell me what's going on?" the officer asked.

Colin opened his mouth, and found he had nothing. He could not say *I was summoned to another world and I helped kill the thing that was burning it and they sent me home and I think I'm in the right state but I'm not sure.* He could not even say the smaller, truer version of it, which was *I don't know how to explain where I've been.*

"I don't know," he said, which was honest. "I don't know what's going on."

The officer studied him. Then he reached for the radio, and in the flat, professional voice men use when they've decided to be calm, he said:

"I've got a possible missing person at the Sunoco off 71. Male, early twenties, disoriented. No ID on him."

And Colin stood there in the dark, in his mended clothes, and listened to the officer call it in—a flat voice on the radio already building a story about him before he'd managed to tell one himself.

He almost said something. He almost corrected it. But he didn't, because the truth was that every version of what he could say was worse than the silence, and he was so tired, and the air was still wrong.

Two years. The woman inside was still watching through the glass, phone held up.

He was home. Probably.

And the air was wrong here too—wrong in the one place it was supposed to be right.
