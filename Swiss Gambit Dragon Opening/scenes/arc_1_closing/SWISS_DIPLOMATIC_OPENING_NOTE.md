# Swiss Diplomatic Opening Note

Continues from `SWISS_BERN_OFFICE_SCENE_NOTE.md` (which ends with the senior FSS officer calling up the chain).

This is a rough scene-direction note for what happens after the Fedpol protects the building and refers the matter upward.

Not locked prose.

## Short Answer

Aiden wins "real" in the office.

Then he discovers that "real" was only the entrance exam.

The actual test the Swiss run is not "is he a wizard."

It is "does the thing he claims to represent satisfy three boxes, one of which cannot even be shown from Earth."

That gap is the diplomatic scene.

So the diplomatic scene is not Aiden convincing anyone.

It is Aiden discovering that the real obstacle was never disbelief, but effective statehood on a world that cannot see the territory.

## The Referral

The FSS officer does not decide anything. He refers.

His report goes up from Building Security to FSS leadership.

Because the words "impossible," "U.S. citizen," and "sovereignty claim" collide in one file, it also flies sideways:
- to the intelligence/analysis side (is this hostile, is this real, is this a threat)
- eventually to the FDFA Directorate of International Law, because that is the unit that owns the legal questions around privileges, immunities, and recognition

The first desk to touch a claim from an unrecognized actor is not the glamour office.
It is the international-law lawyers.

## The Quiet Machinery

Nothing about recognition moves fast, and no one in the building can grant it.

- recognition is a Federal Council political decision, at discretion, routed through the FDFA
- it is measured against the three-element doctrine: population, delimited territory, effective public authority
- Switzerland recognizes states, not governments, and since 1945 it has been doctrinaire about this
- there is no obligation to recognize
- recognition is unilateral, and revocable in practice but legally sticky once granted

This is not background decoration. It is the obstacle.

## The Cold Turn

The next person through the door is not a diplomat in a suit who believes him.

It is a senior FDFA international-law officer, likely trailed by an intelligence or political analyst.

The tone refuses Aiden's vocabulary: no wizard, no magic, only "what you did" and "your claim."

The opening move is the most deflating thing he has heard all night:

### "We accept, for the purpose of this conversation, that what happened in this room cannot be faked. That is not the difficulty. The difficulty is that nothing you have shown us can be recognized by Switzerland, because you have not shown us a territory, and you have shown us authority over nothing we can locate. So tell us what we are actually looking at, and who answers for it."

Aiden has spent the night proving magic.

He has not, and cannot, prove the things that matter to a state:
- a delimited territory on any map
- a public authority that exercises effective power in a way Switzerland can verify
- a responsible counterpart who answers for his power

And Aiden can hold the big picture (rough history, how magic works, the danger the kingdom is in) but is vague on the specifics that matter (exact titles, the legal instrument authorizing him, how the kingdom can be held accountable from Earth).

That vagueness is not a flaw in the scene. It is the point.

### The more precisely he is asked, the more clearly the Swiss see a tired twenty-year-old with real power and no charter.

Which is its own quiet alarm:

a lone man with real magic and no accountable authority is worse than a hoax,
because a hoax can be filed and a state can be negotiated with,
but an unaccountable power has no party to answer.

## The System Rules This Scene Rests On

Locked for now, explored more deeply later:

- teleportation needs an anchor at the destination, and a confirmed reference of it
- Earth is mana-dead, so jumping to Earth takes more careful, more symbol-dense etching than jumping within the kingdom
- the etched circle is a two-way anchor: he jumps out on it, and returns to the same circle with the passenger
- a passenger is possible if he has the mana, and a living body costs more than inert mass
- the cost of this jump is real but quietly absorbed here, partly because the passenger is coming from a mana-rich kingdom and is already mana-soaked; Aiden does not advertise the cost
- the harder problem — moving a dead-mana passenger the other direction — has not been asked yet, and is the thing the Swiss do not yet know to fear
- translation is a script, a spell; it runs through Aiden, and it solves words, not meaning

## The Teleport Demonstration

### 1. The dead end and the offer

The DV officer pushes to the point that defeats Aiden: exact territorial extent, legal instruments, who actually has authority.

Aiden sits with it, then offers instead of answering:

### "I can't give you that. But I can bring someone whose job is to. From [kingdom]. I'll need a room — empty, big enough, one you don't mind me damaging the floor of. I'll teleport him here."

"What."

A beat.

"We should probably record this."

### 2. The room

They move him again: a bare, concrete-floored space, no windows worth the name.

Cold. Not for jokes, not for triage. For a thing that might leave a mark.

Five of them: Aiden, the DV officer, a security/intelligence officer, the recording specialist, one FSS witness. The FSS senior has already handed off and gone.

Each has standing: law, security, documentation, corroboration.

### 3. The almost-start

Aiden crouches, begins to etch, then stops mid-air and deflates.

He pulls out his phone and scrolls through photos, awkwardly, while the room watches.

"Forgot something," he mutters, to himself.

He forgot he is bringing a person, not proof, and needs their face to anchor the search-lock.

He finds the reference image, locks the screen, concentrates, and the phone floats up beside him, angled so he can see it while he works.

The officers are now past "good magician." One of them stops breathing normally.

### 4. The etching, for real

He etches deliberately into the concrete floor.

This is not the panic-circle painted under a rug in Ohio.

It is slower and more symbol-dense, because Earth is mana-dead and jumping to it, and carrying a body back out of it, is the hard direction.

The effort is visible. A fake is effortless. This is not.

### 5. The circle, examined

They drift closer to look at the circle: geometry that should not be, light sitting wrong in the grooves.

Aiden catches them and starts to explain, and for the first time all night he sounds homey.

The outer ring stabilizes spatial reference. The second array holds temporal lock so one arrives when one left. The inner set lets him carry mass instead of just himself.

He nerds out, almost childlike. The threat dissolves into a kid explaining a hobby.

Then he sees their faces and remembers where he is.

He centers himself.

"Please move back. I'll begin."

### 6. The pose, interrupted

He steps to the circle's edge and the robe starts to move: a slow lift, in still air, like fabric remembering something.

The glove-hand flexes.

Camera guy: "Wait, sorry — face this way?"

Aiden blinks out of the trance.

"Oh. Right. Sorry."

He re-poses, turns to the lens, tucks the robe straight with both hands, waits.

Thumbs up.

He chants — low, syllables from no language in the room — and is simply gone.

The circle goes dark. A pressure-pop in everyone's ears, like the room exhaled.

### 7. The absence

Nobody moves.

They are afraid to step into the circle, so they do not.

The camera keeps rolling on nothing.

Someone checks the other door.

There is no flash like Ohio. Just the awful ordinary fact of a man having stood there and now not.

Minutes pass.

### 8. The return

The circle lights, sudden and total.

Everyone takes five steps back without deciding to.

Two of them now.

Aiden, visibly wrong: sweat-soaked, grey, breathing like a fever.

Beside him, an impossible person in medieval court dress, looking yanked out of his own world mid-sentence.

Aiden, hoarse:

### "This is [name], [title]. He can speak on behalf of [kingdom]."

The diplomat stares around the concrete room, then at the DV officer, then performs the most absurd thing anyone has ever seen: he recovers, centers himself, and offers a formal diplomatic greeting, as if being teleported to another world were an unusual commute.

He speaks, and it is not a language anyone in the room knows.

Aiden, from the chair, remembering:

"Oh — right. Give me a second."

Then he translates.

The DV officer answers with protocol, because that, somehow, is the only available response.

### 9. The spent ending

Formal diplomacy begins across an etched concrete floor.

Aiden, having produced a nation out of nowhere and pulled a man through a wall in reality, simply sits on the nearest chair, spent, and does not participate in his own moment.

## The Mage Arrives

### 10. The mage is fetched

Aiden was never the one who teleports people; a kingdom mage is.

So he makes one last crossing: a short jump back, a single "we need you here," and he does not return alone.

The mage comes back with him — the teleport specialist, the one whose actual job the night's travel has been impersonating.

Aiden is at roughly 90 percent: grey, slow, but still here, still grounding the scene.

### 11. The something that is missing

The mage arrives on Earth and it is wrong.

Not painful, and not lethal. Not tired.

Missing.

She is a body that passively generates and passively drinks mana, and Earth gives nothing back.

So she stands there, breathing fine, and keeps almost-flexing, and nothing answers.

It is like being somewhere that does not return the pressure you are used to; air with no weight behind it.

She cannot put it into words and does not need to.

The Swiss watch a being who can do what Aiden does visibly suffer just from existing here, and that scares them more than any demonstration.

Their world is a starved place to her, and it is on tape now.

### 12. The smug arrival, grounded

She introduces herself, and she is, for a moment, slightly smug with the serious people in the room.

Aiden, exhausted, has to ground her:

"Please be serious."

She is. Mostly.

### 13. The scolding, and the harder truth

She looks at Aiden's rough teleport circle and corrects it, not as a master correcting a schoolkid, but as someone hitting the same wall he did.

The ground here gives nothing back.

So the correction is earth-side, specific, the kind of thing you only know once you try:

"You were supposed to double the anchor array. This ground doesn't answer back."

Then she, too, underestimates Earth, and discovers it is harder than she expected, not easier.

The same wall, twice. Both of them running face-first into a mana-dead world.

### 14. Yes, but

The Swiss question lands: can you bring us there?

The answer, now, is possible in principle.

Not tonight. Not safely. Not in the numbers they would want. Not without preparation.

Moving dead-mana living bodies across that jump is the unexplored dangerous edge of the whole system, and no one in the room — not even the mage — wants to be first to test it on Swiss officials.

So the answer stays: yes, but.

A project, not a revelation.

## The Encampment

Five hours later. The deferred reward beat.

The language barrier is solved — translation is a spell, and it runs through Aiden like a low-grade hum he no longer fully notices.

What remains after words are solved is meaning, and that is the part no spell does.

### 1. Cold open, mid-conversation

The scene opens already deep in it, hours in, and the subject is not magic but administration.

The paladin, politely, about a point of order:

### "But the [name] Church is a separate branch from the kingdom. Its own organization, its own hierarchy. I would say it stands apart."

Aiden, translating, hoarse with the drag of it:

### "Yes, but we can't count them as the same jurisdiction as the kingdom —"

He coughs once.

The healer is already leaning in:

"Aiden, are you alright? Should I cast stamina enhancement?"

Aiden, not looking up:

"No. That's fine. Just — can you help me explain how the Church works?"

He is not sick. He is tired, and the difference is the whole scene.

### 2. The wide pan

The reader should now be shown what "five hours" actually became.

It is the holding room, transformed.

An improvised table. Folding chairs. A power strip. A laptop near the wall. Takeaway coffee gone cold.

The etched two-way anchor is still there, under all of them, and nobody thinks about stepping on it anymore.

Six mages argue with Earth engineers about the crossing problem.

Somewhere a wyvern-hide map is being propped against a modern projector screen, and it will not stay flat.

More diplomats than Aiden has ever seen in one room.

The envoy is still going, somewhere in the middle of it, tireless in the way of professionals.

### 2A. The room vibe

The room was never meant for this.

It was chosen for one interview, and now it holds an incident, so everything in it is being asked to do a job it was not built for.

#### Bodies

Around twenty-five to thirty people, in a space made for maybe five.

It does not fill with a snap. It swells, as each new arrival comes in baffled, gets a thirty-second sotto-voce briefing from whoever is nearest, goes pale, and makes a phone call that brings someone else.

Two sides who do not read each other's rank, because rank means different things in each:

- the kingdom reads by danger and access, not title; the party can walk up to the king and talk to him casually, which is its own kind of rank that no title expresses
- the Swiss read by form: protocol, accreditation, who is on paper the authority

So each side over-respects the wrong person and under-reads the right one, and both are half-correct.

##### The kingdom pecking order, in substance

- Aiden, at the top, by access and dependence: war hero, party leader, the king's close ear, and the irreplaceable node to Earth. In kingdom terms he outranks the envoy, even though he is not, legally, anything.
- The hero party as a group: the Church emissaries (the healer and paladin are very high), the singularity-mage, and the last-resort special-force core. Respected not as functionaries but as the people who keep the kingdom alive.
- The envoy: the formal statecraft layer, several rungs of informal power below. The envoy is who you send to be correct. The party is who you send to survive.

##### What the Swiss see, in form

The accredited diplomat must be the authority, and the translator is staff.

So the Swiss defer to the envoy, the envoy defers to Aiden, and neither the Swiss nor Aiden can correct it without revealing how precarious Aiden's performed standing actually is.

The paladin and healer being Church emissaries is the latent bomb: they answer to a different sovereign than the one the Swiss think they are negotiating with, and no one has explained that yet.

#### Furniture

Two or three tables pushed together, none matching.

Not enough chairs, so people stand. The paladin will not sit on a folding chair, on some unspoken scale of dignity, and stays near the wall.

The healer has produced a better chair for Aiden from somewhere, and no one asks where it came from.

#### The map problem

Two cartographies side by side on the same board, and no bridge between them:

a rough map of the kingdom drawn in marker, taped next to a printed satellite image of Bern.

#### Power and climate

One floor cable taped down, feeding a strip that is now full.

Climate control set for an ordinary day, which is wrong for everyone. The kingdom party finds the air stale and keeps almost-flexing their hands, the "something is missing" made ambient.

#### The food gradient

The Earth side does not eat. Too tense. The sandwiches and coffee on their half of the table go cold, untouched.

The kingdom side raids the table on sight, because they have had Aiden's smuggled Earth food before and to them it is a treat, not a threat.

The paladin tries to be dignified about it and fails. The healer is methodical. A mage demolishes a shrink-wrapped sandwich while arguing a diagram.

The mundane is the exotic to them, and it shows.

#### The empty center

The etched circle is still there, and it is the only empty space left.

Nobody steps on it. Chairs have been angled, subtly, away from it, the way chairs angle away from a body.

### 3. The structural texture

This is the payoff the recognition beat was always pointing toward.

The Swiss are discovering that their new counterpart is not one coherent state.

It is a nest: the kingdom, the Church as a separate jurisdiction, and whatever else Aiden has half-translated tonight, all with claims and boundaries that do not map cleanly onto anything in their doctrine.

Aiden is the only one who can translate the meaning, not just the words.

That is why, at 90 percent and sinking, he is still the load-bearing piece of the room.

### 4. The king's name in the air

Not an arrival. A whisper.

A line, somewhere, that this now goes higher than mages and engineers and even envoys.

That recognition is not a thing anyone in this building can grant, because it is a sovereign matter, and sovereigns may have to actually speak.

Nobody says it directly.

It just hangs there, over the busy calm.

### 5. The party, assembled by interruption

The full roster is here now, and the reader learns it the way Aiden does: not by introduction, but by having people show up mid-sentence.

Aiden is still half-explaining the Church when the paladin finishes the thought for him, already standing, already waiting to contribute:

"The hierarchy of the Church stands apart from the throne. It is a matter of doctrine, not of loyalty."

Aiden, exhausted, gestures:

"Right. What he said."

The healer is suddenly there too, hovering, having long since stopped asking permission.

And somewhere in the room, the ranger stands over the rough map of the kingdom, walked through its geography for the Swiss, tracing borders that exist on no Earth chart.

The effect is not introduction.

It is the party having reassembled around him, piece by piece, on the far side of a wall in reality, and no one narrating it.

### 6. The teleport argument: four is too much

The Swiss have asked for a delegation to cross and see the kingdom themselves: four people.

A diplomat, to meet the king.

A scientist.

A security man, the army-general type.

And a cameraman, because whatever happens, they will not go without proof of their own.

The six mages cluster around the etched circle and tell them why not.

Mage, blunt:

"Four is too much."

Swiss rep:

"Four what?"

Mage:

"Four Earth people. I do not like it. The last time we sent a [kingdom] golem here, it broke the circle."

Aiden cuts in, correcting the wording, not the fact:

"Don't say broke. The circle couldn't hold the load."

Mage, not caring:

"It broke. And the golem broke too."

Swiss rep, going very still:

"Aiden. What does she mean by broke. Is that a mistranslation."

Mage, waving a hand, already a step past it:

"It should be fine with them here. The anchor is doubled now. Probably."

Swiss rep, louder:

"WHAT DOES BROKE MEAN, AIDEN."

Aiden opens his mouth to explain it properly: that broke meant the golem's mana-crystal cracked, that it arrived inert but whole, like an appliance dead on arrival in the post, that no one was harmed because there was no one inside it to harm.

And from across the room, perfectly timed, the contesting diplomat:

"Aiden. What does twenty-four carat mean. Our mine should be pure enough. Twenty-four is the purest, correct?"

Aiden makes his choice. Money is real, and the golem is past.

"I'll be back, [name]," he says to the Swiss rep.

Who is still silently mouthing broke.

### 7. The un-explanation, held

The Swiss rep is left alone with the word broke, next to a mage who has already lost interest and is drawing a different diagram.

The joke is that broke was almost certainly survivable: a cracked crystal, a failed shipment, an engineering footnote.

But no one in the room will ever tell them that, because Aiden got intercepted, and the mage does not understand why broke would alarm anyone.

So four Swiss officials now believe, on the strength of one un-translated word, that being teleported may mean being broken.

And the mage, meaning to reassure, will only make it worse later, without ever noticing.

### 8. The spent ending, again, differently

Aiden is present but hollow.

Cleaned up, healed at least once, handed food at some point, and still somehow not fully in the room.

He has been absorbed entirely into the machinery he forced into motion.

The world he opened will not stop producing schematics.

And he, translating, does not participate in his own moment — except now it is not a single night, but his whole position, and there is no chair far enough away from it.

## The Crossing

This is the arc's climax: Swiss officials actually step through into the kingdom.

### 1. The cuts

Someone has to be dropped, because the mage's ceiling is real.

The scientist is the one who gets cut.

Which is the quiet insult and the Earth-priority in one lineup change: no room for the person whose job is to understand, but room for the person whose job is to record.

So the delegation is three now:

- the diplomat, to meet the king
- the security man, the army-general type
- the cameraman — the one who asked what broke means, and whose camera goes with him

### 2. The broke question, one more time

As the six mages gather around the circle, the cameraman asks it again, because it is his body now, and his gear:

"Aiden. What did broke mean. Before I go."

Aiden opens his mouth.

And the mages begin to chant, and he is pulled into position, and the answer never comes.

### 3. The ceremony, watched

The Earth side stops what it is doing and watches.

Not in terror, this time. In the raw curiosity of watching a process instead of its result.

The circle fills with light, and the light has structure — a pattern, a pressure, syllables in no language, and the strange lift of the air.

The Swiss do not run.

They watch the door they have argued about for five hours finally, actually open.

### 4. The crossing

There is a pressure-pop, like the room exhaled.

Then the safe, ordinary fact of arrival.

They are in the kingdom.

### 5. Broke, paid off

The cameraman finds his main camera dead.

Not the way a dropped camera is dead. The lens has a crack through it, a fine clean fracture, like something stressed it from the wrong direction. Or the sensor is simply fried, shot through with a bloom that no light made.

He stares at it.

"Oh."

A long beat.

"Oh, that's what he meant by broke."

The mage, offhand, without looking up from her recalculations:

"Hm? Oh. Yes. You brought the large camera. I forgot to calculate the large camera."

The cameraman pulls out his phone instead — unbroken, mundane, already recording:

"Should've brought the scientist."

### 6. The inverted world

The real payoff is what the Swiss feel next.

They have spent hours watching the kingdom party almost-flex their hands in Bern, complaining of something missing.

Now it is reversed.

The air here has weight. A presence. The Swiss feel it as a mild charge, a warmth behind the eyes, a sense that the room is more full than it looks.

They were the ones from the dead place, and they did not know it until now.

Aiden, watching them feel it, says nothing.

It is the first time in five hours he has looked like he is home.

## The Kingdom Welcoming Scene

This is the arrival beat, and the first time Earth dignitaries stand in the fantasy world.

Not locked prose.

### 1. The arrival

The three step out of nothing, and the first thing that registers is not architecture or people.

It is the air, already established: it has weight, a warmth behind the eyes, a room more full than it looks.

The cameraman swings his phone toward everything, and even that tiny lens seems to film through warm water.

The diplomat holds form. Barely.

The security man carries the Swiss rifle out of habitual duty, because they are entering an unknown world and he would no more go unarmed than unbreathing.

### 2. The rifle is a non-event

Nobody in the kingdom reacts to the rifle as a weapon.

They look at it the way the Swiss might look at a curious antique: interested, unbothered.

That unnerves the security man more than being disarmed would.

He is standing armed, and somehow less safe for it.

Across the arrivals, the diplomat shoots the security man a small look that says, silently: a rifle, really, into a sovereign's court.

The security man's face answers, silently: we walked through a hole in reality. I am not unarmed.

The two of them do not say it.

They do not have to.

### 3. The welcome, overdone

The kingdom wants to receive them and has no protocol for it, so it overdoes it with sincerity.

The herald comes first — not the king, not yet — and bows too deep and speaks a title none of them recognize, and then, because they are Earth visitors, does it wrong in a very human way:

- over-serving, apologizing for the wrong things
- offering refreshment that is not quite food
- bowing again, as if a third bow might fix it

The envoy, already present, steps back and yields. This is not his moment.

The hero party forms a loose line behind Aiden: healer, paladin, ranger, tank. Not a guard. Just the people who are always there.

### 4. The three reactions, in parallel

The cameraman stops being a professional within thirty seconds.

He keeps the phone raised but forgets to aim it, because he is seeing, and the awe is almost religious: small noises, half-spoken words, the wonder of a man watching a miracle become merely a place.

The security man cannot stop doing threat math.

He is cataloguing exits, hands, the way the guards stand, and slowly realizing the calculation will never finish, because his training has no field for this.

The diplomat is the one who breaks, and it is the most important beat.

Not visibly. He holds composure perfectly.

But at some point he says something in the wrong register, or forgets a formula, or goes so still that it is clear he is recalculating his entire world in real time.

He is not awed and not afraid.

He is professionally unraveling, and that is different from both.

### 5. Aiden is home, and catches the unraveling

Aiden stands slightly apart, and for the first time in five hours he is not the bridge being leaned on.

He lets himself be home for exactly two seconds.

Then he notices the diplomat unraveling, and quietly moves to catch him — not with words, just by being there, translating the welcome into something the Swiss can stand upright in.

### 6. The capper

The herald finally asks, with the formality of someone raised entirely on ritual:

### "Which of you speaks for your kingdom?"

And all three of them, without thinking, turn to look at Aiden.

Aiden, exhausted, home, is already walking forward to translate the answer.

## The Provisional Recognition

This is the Arc-1 resolution: the thing Aiden fled Ohio for, finally put in front of him.

And it must land as an anticlimax that he predicted, not a shock.

### 1. Not called recognition

The word never appears clean.

What the Swiss hand Aiden is a provisional framework for contact, or a memorandum of provisional status: language deliberately drained of anything that sounds like statehood.

No wizard, no kingdom, no recognition.

Only "the subject," "the represented entity," "the counterpart in question."

### 2. Aiden cannot read it

He looks at the page and the words will not hold still.

Not defeat. Exhaustion. He can read a sentence and lose it by the third clause.

"I can't. Just — tell me what it says. Plain."

### 3. The diplomat translates the state to the boy

The diplomat reads it for him, in the plainest words he has, one line at a time.

He does not soften it. His kindness is in the clarity:

"This says we keep talking to you. That is all it commits to."

"It says you exist to us, provisionally. That is not the same as saying the kingdom is a state."

"It says nothing here obligates Switzerland beyond continuing the conversation. Nothing here is recognition. Do you understand what it gives you?"

The de facto / de jure line, made explicit:

- de facto: we acknowledge you exist and will deal with you, provisionally, because ceasing would be worse
- de jure: we recognize you as a sovereign state — which this does not do, which only the Federal Council could do, and which the page does not even gesture toward

### 4. Aiden's landing

Aiden, exhausted, finds the one honest thing in it:

### "A door that isn't closed. Good. That's a start."

Not a victory.

The confirmation that the hard road is open and barely passable — which is more than he had in Ohio.

It is the burnout thesis in a single breath: he already knew it would be provisional, conditional, legal to the point of insulting. He came anyway.

### 5. The signing, dessicated

The moment should be historic, and instead it is human, and that is the point.

The cameraman wanders off history and into hobby, photographing a guard and asking him to hold his axe sideways.

The commander grabs him and re-aims him at the table, because the commander alone understands the recording is the leverage:

"This. This is what you photograph."

The signing itself is absurdly anticlimactic:

- Aiden has no surface to write on, so he borrows the paladin's back and signs against it
- half-dignified, half-ridiculous, and it is the accidental hero photo of the night
- then the countersignatures: the diplomat, the envoy, and the herald signing on behalf of the king, because the king has not come down yet

The document went as high as it could tonight, not as high as it ultimately must.

## The America Shadow

The cold closer.

No phone. Aiden's phone was never the connection.

The connection is procedural, and it has been accumulating all night:

- Aiden was declared missing, with an oddly-worded, over-urgent circulation out: do not approach, notify immediately, may be accompanied by unknown associates
- the wording is strange because the Americans cannot write what they actually saw
- that circulation reaches the Swiss passively, through the consular and liaison channels
- someone runs a routine check against Swiss systems because the urgency is unusual
- and it hits the passport entry from earlier tonight
- he was at their desk three hours ago
- and the passport itself is already strange: a real U.S. citizen whose travel history is nearly flat, a Japan holiday years ago, then nothing international, and no recorded entry into Switzerland at all

A quiet word reaches the room:

### "The Americans are asking about a missing citizen. We had him three hours ago. They are going to want him back."

The room's temperature drops again.

Not because Aiden is too strange to believe now. Because he has just been reclassified in a single sentence.

Before: a credible petitioner.

After: a contested asset that a superpower will soon try to extract.

The recognition he just won is, the instant it exists, the reason he is now about to be fought over.

"Good, that's a start," and then the superpower arrives to test whether the start holds.

## Arc 1 Closes Here

The provisional recognition is won, provisional and de facto, never called recognition, signed against a paladin's back while a cameraman photographs a guard's axe.

And the America shadow breaks through over the freshly-won shelter, turning Aiden from guest to contested asset in a single spoken sentence.

That is the closing image of the arc.

## Continues In Next Beats

The next surface: the actual American arrival — how and when Washington moves physically to reclaim its missing citizen, and what the Swiss decide to do with an asset they now have to protect or surrender.

Also unresolved and deliberate:

- the king still has not come down; the audience with a true sovereign has not yet happened
- the healer and the paladin are Church emissaries, and answer to a different sovereign than the one the Swiss think they are negotiating with, and no one has explained that yet
- Aiden's "Earth diplomat" title was never formally issued, and no one has probed it yet
